## Context

Cold-storage chambers and HVAC equipment are large, layout-sensitive assemblies. Components have to be arranged around the chamber's own geometry and around real space constraints, and the assembly sequence matters as much as the parts list.

My role spanned manufacturing support, configuration control and problem resolution — the work that determines whether a design is actually producible at rate.

## Layout under constraint

I supported manufacturing and assembly by **adapting component layouts to chamber design and space constraints**. In equipment of this type the enclosure is often fixed first and everything else has to be accommodated inside it, so layout becomes an exercise in sequencing: what has to go in first, what needs service access afterwards, and what cannot be reached once something else is installed.

## Time studies and the 25% reduction

The cycle-time work started with measurement rather than opinion.

I conducted **time studies to identify production bottlenecks** — establishing where time was actually being consumed rather than where it was assumed to be. Bottleneck analysis reliably contradicts intuition: the slow-looking station is frequently not the constraint, and the real constraint is often a wait state nobody was recording.

Against those findings I developed **standard operating procedures**. SOPs matter for cycle time because unstandardised work is variable work, and variable work forces every downstream station to buffer against the worst case. Codifying the best-known method removes that variance.

Together, the time studies and SOPs contributed to a **25% reduction in manufacturing cycle time**.

## BOM and configuration control

I **managed and interpreted BOMs** and supported **first-article validation**, ensuring accurate product configuration and manufacturing documentation.

I also implemented **ECO/ECN design changes** — updating BOMs, drawings and manufacturing processes so that changes propagated completely. An engineering change that reaches the drawing but not the BOM, or the BOM but not the work instruction, produces exactly the kind of configuration drift that surfaces months later as an unexplained field failure.

## Root cause analysis

When manufacturing issues arose I performed **RCA and 5-Why analysis** and drove corrective actions, reducing defects and improving equipment reliability.

The discipline of 5-Why is that it forces past the first plausible explanation. The first answer is usually a symptom; the useful answer is the process or design condition that allowed the symptom to occur, because that is the only level at which a fix stays fixed.

## Outcome

- **25% reduction** in manufacturing cycle time
- SOPs and time studies established as **standard practice** for bottleneck identification
- Accurate configuration maintained through **BOM management and ECO/ECN control**
- Defects reduced and reliability improved through **structured RCA**

## What I'd carry forward

Process improvement without measurement is guesswork. The 25% came from time studies first and changes second — and the SOPs were what kept the improvement from decaying once attention moved elsewhere.
