## Context

Flight control cable pulleys and their guide brackets sit in a load path where the consequences of a bad fit are not cosmetic. They also have to be produced repeatably, at rate, on manufacturing lines that were increasingly automated.

My work covered both halves of that: the part design itself, and the design decisions that determine whether a part can be produced efficiently.

## Design

Pulleys and mechanical guide brackets were modelled in **Siemens NX and SolidWorks**, developed against aerospace manufacturing and assembly requirements rather than in isolation.

The design intent had to survive contact with the production floor, which meant working closely with manufacturing and supplier teams throughout — not handing a model over at the end and waiting for the objections.

## Tolerance stack-up analysis

Most assembly defects are tolerance problems that nobody caught on the drawing.

I performed **GD&T tolerance stack-up analysis** across the assemblies to confirm that parts produced anywhere within their stated tolerance envelope would still assemble and function. This served two purposes:

1. **Manufacturability** — confirming the tolerances specified were ones the process could actually hold, rather than tight numbers written defensively
2. **Defect elimination** — identifying stack conditions that would have produced interference or excessive play at the extremes of the tolerance range

Catching these on paper is orders of magnitude cheaper than catching them in first-article inspection, and cheaper still than catching them in the field.

## Optimising for automated production

The **8% manufacturing cycle-time improvement** came from optimising the designs specifically for automated production cells.

Designing for an automated cell is a different problem to designing for a bench. Part geometry has to support consistent presentation, gripping and orientation; features have to be reachable by the tooling; and the design has to avoid the conditions that cause a cell to stop, because an automated line's throughput is dominated by its interruptions rather than its nominal rate.

## Tooling and fixtures

I designed and evaluated **tooling and fixtures** to improve assembly efficiency and support production readiness. Fixtures are where design intent becomes repeatable: a good fixture makes the correct assembly the easy one and the incorrect assembly awkward or impossible.

## Risk methods

Working with manufacturing and supplier teams, I applied **DFMEA and DFSS methods** to improve product quality and reduce design and production risk. Used properly, DFMEA is a structured way of asking "how does this fail, how would we know, and what does it cost us" early enough that the answers can still change the design.

## Outcome

- **8% improvement** in manufacturing cycle time through design optimisation for automated cells
- **Assembly defects eliminated** through GD&T tolerance stack-up analysis
- Tooling and fixtures delivered to support **production readiness**
- Design and production risk reduced via **DFMEA / DFSS** with manufacturing and supplier teams

## What I'd carry forward

The cycle-time gain did not come from making the part faster to machine in isolation — it came from designing it around the constraints of the cell that would produce it. Manufacturing efficiency is a design input, and it has to be present from the first sketch to be worth anything.
