## Premise

In-space resource utilisation depends on getting material somewhere useful. A near-Earth asteroid already carries the mass; the engineering question is what it costs to move it into an orbit where it can be reached — and when that cost is lowest.

This research builds a computational framework to answer that question quantitatively for candidate near-Earth asteroids.

## Real data, not toy inputs

The model runs on **real orbital elements and ephemeris data retrieved from the NASA Jet Propulsion Laboratory Small-Body Database** for near-Earth asteroids.

This matters. A propagator validated against invented initial conditions tells you about your integrator; a propagator running on published ephemerides tells you about the actual objects, and lets results be checked against an external reference.

## The framework

I developed a framework in **Python** to model and visualise two-dimensional asteroid trajectories from first principles of celestial mechanics.

The core loop is orbit propagation: given a state, advance it under gravitational dynamics and observe where the object goes. On top of that sits the question the research actually cares about — **where in that trajectory does an applied impulse buy the most orbital change for the least propellant.**

## Finding the intervention window

Orbital transfers are exquisitely sensitive to timing. The same ΔV applied at different points in an orbit produces wildly different results, because the change in orbital energy depends on where in the orbit the impulse is applied.

The simulation propagates candidate orbits to **identify optimal intervention windows** and determine the **minimum ΔV required** to redirect a candidate asteroid into a stable orbit in the vicinity of Earth, the Moon, or Mars.

## Optimisation and stability

Applying **numerical methods and optimisation techniques**, the framework minimises propulsion requirements — but under a constraint that makes the problem substantially harder: the resulting orbit has to remain **stable over the long term**.

A minimum-ΔV solution that places an asteroid into a configuration that decays or escapes within a few years is not a solution. Long-term stability is therefore treated as a constraint on the optimisation rather than as something checked afterwards.

## Engineering simulation

Beyond the orbital mechanics, I performed **engineering simulations in ANSYS** to evaluate force application strategies, orbital stability and mission feasibility.

This is the bridge from astrodynamics to hardware: the trajectory analysis says what impulse is required and when, but whether that impulse can be delivered to a real body — how force is applied, and what it does to the object — is a mechanical problem.

## Visualisation

The framework produces **2-D visualisations of orbital transfers**, which allow trajectory alternatives to be compared directly and support mission planning. The interactive simulator on the [research page](/research/) is a browser implementation of the same Keplerian propagation the framework is built on.

## Status

Ongoing since May 2024. Current work continues on expanding the candidate set and refining the optimisation under long-term stability constraints.
